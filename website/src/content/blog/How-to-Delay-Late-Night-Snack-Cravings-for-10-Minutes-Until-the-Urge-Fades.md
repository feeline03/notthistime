## The Most Impossible Few Minutes of Your Day

Here's the strange thing about a craving: it feels like it will last forever. At 11:30 p.m., it feels less like a thought and more like a *command*.

But cravings aren't commands. They're **waves.**

They build, they crest, and, if you don't act on them, they tend to fall. In the world of habit change, this idea is called **urge surfing**: instead of fighting the urge or obeying it, you notice it, stay with it, and let it pass the way a surfer rides a wave until it breaks.

For many people, the most intense part of a craving lasts only a few minutes. That's the whole secret behind the "wait 10 minutes" trick. You're not saying "never." You're saying *"not yet."*

The problem is that those few minutes are exactly where most of us break. Because in the moment, there's nothing between the urge and the action. No friction. No pause. Just you, a hungry-feeling brain, and a very convenient bag of chips.

The good news is that a few minutes is a small enough window to work with.

## Why Your Brain Isn't on Your Side After Dark

Let's get one thing out of the way: **this is not a willpower failure.** Your brain, at night, is a very different machine than the one that got you through your morning.

**1. Your "brakes" are tired.**
The front part of your brain (the prefrontal cortex) helps you plan, weigh consequences and say no. It does its best work when you're rested and calm. After a long day of decisions, stress, screens and holding it together, and especially when you're short on sleep, those brakes get weaker. The part of you that says "we'll regret this tomorrow" is quieter than it was at breakfast.

**2. Your reward system doesn't clock out.**
Meanwhile, the brain's reward circuitry (the system that makes you *want* things) is highly responsive to cues: the kitchen, the couch, the phone, the quiet. Dopamine isn't only about pleasure. It's a lot about **anticipation**, the "ooh, that would feel good" pull. And for many of us, snacks have become a reliable way to feel better after a hard day.

**3. The evening itself works against you.**
Research on the body's daily rhythms suggests that appetite and reward can shift in the evening, which may make high-calorie, comforting foods feel more appealing at night. Add tiredness, stress, boredom or loneliness, and you've got a perfect storm.

So when you think, *"Why can't I just stop?"*, the more accurate question might be: *"Why am I trying to resist with my weakest tool at the hardest hour?"*

**Your brain isn't broken. It's being asked to do a hard job at the worst possible time.**

And that, strangely, is good news. If it's about timing and tools, not character, then it can be fixed with timing and tools.

## 3 Tiny Ways to Get Through the Wave

You don't need a morning-person routine or an hour of meditation. At 11:30 p.m., you need things that are **physical, tiny, and almost impossible to overthink.**

### 1. The 90-Second Brush Break

**Go brush your teeth. Properly. Floss if you're feeling fancy.**

Why it works:

- It's a **physical change of state.** You've moved, changed rooms, and given your hands something to do.
- It creates a **sensory shift.** Minty-fresh mouth and food don't mix well, so snacking suddenly feels less appealing.
- It sends a **"kitchen is closed"** signal to yourself.

It's not magic, and you can still eat afterward if you truly want to. But brushing buys you a small, clean pause, and pauses are where the wave starts to fall.

### 2. The Physical Distance Trick

**Add friction between you and the food.**

The less effort it takes, the more automatic the habit. So make it just slightly harder:

- **Move the snacks** to a high shelf, the back of the pantry, or an opaque container.
- **Put your phone in another room** (or at least log out of the delivery apps) so your thumb can't autopilot to checkout.
- **Sit somewhere that isn't the kitchen or a place you usually snack.** Change the scene, change the script.

Friction doesn't need to be a wall. It just needs to be a **speed bump**, enough to interrupt the autopilot and give your thinking brain a chance to catch up.

### 3. The 3-Minute Urge Wave

This is the core move. **Set a timer for three minutes, and make one promise: "I'll decide after the timer."**

During those three minutes:

- **Name the urge.** Say it to yourself: *"This is a craving. This is a wave."*
- **Check the feeling underneath.** Tired? Stressed? Lonely? Bored? One word is enough.
- **Breathe slowly.** Let your exhale run a little longer than your inhale, and drop your shoulders.
- **Notice where you feel it** (chest? stomach? hands?) and watch whether it rises, plateaus, or softens.

When the timer ends, ask: *"Is it still as strong as it was?"*

If it has softened, wonderful. You just rode a wave. If it hasn't, you can extend the pause to ten minutes, or, if you decide to eat, do it **deliberately:** sitting down, on a plate, without shame.

**Eating on purpose is not the same as failing.** Breaking the autopilot is the win.
