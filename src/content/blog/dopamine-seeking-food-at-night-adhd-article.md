## The Wanting That Isn't Hunger

Here's what makes this pattern so confusing: **it often doesn't feel like hunger.** It feels like restlessness, boredom, a vague "I need something" that your brain translates, within about four seconds, into *snack.*

You might notice:

- You were focused all evening, then suddenly can't settle once the tasks are done.
- The craving is for something very specific and very stimulating: crunchy, sweet, salty, intense.
- You eat quickly, barely tasting it, and feel flat or guilty afterwards.
- The next night, the same loop plays out again.

Many people with ADHD describe days that are a constant effort to push through tasks that don't feel rewarding. Then comes the quiet of the evening. No deadlines, no demands, and a brain that has been running on effort all day, now looking for a reward.

**It isn't that you "lack discipline." It's that your brain is asking for stimulation, and food is the quickest, most reliable way to get it.**

## Why Nighttime Is Rough on an ADHD Brain

Let's take the self-blame off the table. It isn't supported by how this works.

**1. The "brakes" get tired.**
The front part of your brain (the prefrontal cortex) helps with planning, weighing consequences and saying no. In ADHD, these executive functions already take more effort to maintain. After a day of pushing through tasks, switching attention and managing impulses, there's often less left in the tank. By late evening, the voice that says "you'll regret this" is quiet.

**2. The reward system is still running, and it's hungry for stimulation.**
ADHD is commonly linked to differences in how the brain handles dopamine, the chemical involved in motivation, reward and **anticipation.** The popular shorthand is "low dopamine," but the reality is more nuanced: it's often more about how the brain *seeks, registers and sustains* reward than about a simple shortage. In practical terms, many people with ADHD find ordinary, low-stimulation moments hard to tolerate and are drawn to quick, high-reward options.

**3. Food is fast, cheap and always available.**
Salty, sweet and crunchy foods deliver an immediate hit of sensation and reward, with almost no effort. For a brain looking for stimulation, the kitchen is the nearest source. Add in tiredness, stress, or medication wearing off in the evening for some people, and the pull gets stronger.

So when you think, *"Why can't I just stop?"*, a kinder and more accurate question is: **"Why am I asking my most worn-out brain systems to resist the most convenient reward, at the hardest hour?"**

You're not broken. Your brain is looking for what it needs, and it's picking the nearest tool. The good news? **If it's a stimulation-seeking loop, you can offer it other options, and you can slow it down.**

## 3 Small Ways to Redirect the Loop

You don't need a long routine. With an ADHD brain at 11:30 p.m., anything complicated gets abandoned. These are **small, physical and fast.**

### 1. Build a "Dopamine Menu" of Non-Food Options

Instead of fighting the craving for stimulation, **give it somewhere else to go.** Write a short list *in advance* (not at midnight) of quick things that offer sensation, novelty or reward without involving food. Keep it somewhere visible, like your phone's notes or a sticky note on the fridge.

A few ideas to start with:

- **Brush your teeth for 90 seconds.** The minty sensation makes snacking less appealing, and it signals "kitchen's closed."
- **Put on one energetic song** and move around the room.
- **Take a warm shower** or wash your face with warm water.
- **Text a friend** a meme, or a voice note.
- **Do a 5-minute tidy sprint,** one surface only.
- **Play a short, satisfying game** or puzzle with a clear end.
- **Step outside** for fresh air and look at the sky.
- **Make a warm drink** and sit with it somewhere that isn't the kitchen.

The point isn't to find the perfect replacement. It's to **give your brain choices,** because a craving with only one option is much harder to ignore.

### 2. Change the Environment

An ADHD brain is strongly shaped by what's in front of it. So make the autopilot path a little harder:

- **Move tempting foods** out of sight: a high shelf, the back of a cupboard, an opaque container.
- **Log out of delivery apps,** or move them off your home screen.
- **Set up your "dopamine menu" items** where you'll see them: headphones by the couch, a puzzle on the table.

These don't need to be perfect walls. They're **speed bumps,** and for an impulsive moment, even a small one can be enough to catch your attention.

### 3. Ride the 3-Minute Wave

This is the core move. **Cravings tend to rise, crest and fall, often within a few minutes if you don't feed them.** This is the idea behind *urge surfing.* You're not saying "never." You're saying "**not yet.**"

When the urge hits:

1. **Start a 3-minute pause** before you go near the fridge.
2. **Name it:** *"This is a stimulation craving. It's a wave."*
3. **Check underneath:** bored? tired? restless? stressed?
4. **Pick one item from your dopamine menu,** so your brain has somewhere to go besides the kitchen.

After three minutes, ask: *"Is it as strong as it was?"* If it has softened, you've just ridden a wave. If it hasn't, you can extend the pause, or eat **on purpose:** sitting down, on a plate, without shame.

**Eating deliberately is not failing. Breaking the autopilot is the win.**
